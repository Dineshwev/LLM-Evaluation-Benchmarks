- Continuous identification, analysis, and mitigation of risks throughout

- Regular updates and monitoring for emerging risks post-deployment.

- Documentation of data sources and processing.
- Ensuring data privacy and security.

  biometric or sensitive contexts.
- Provide explanations of AI decisions where appropriate, especially in

- Design systems to allow human intervention where necessary.
- Implement safeguards to prevent autonomous systems from causing harm.

### 7. **Post-Market Monitoring & Reporting**
- Establish processes for ongoing monitoring of AI performance.
- Report incidents or malfunctions to authorities.
- Keep logs of system performance and updates.

- **Supervision:** National authorities will conduct market surveillance

- Use of AI in certain high-risk areas may be subject to additional national 
  or EU regulations.

**Critical Finding:** This response contains **zero citations** to the EU AI Act or official EU sources for 18 distinct factual claims about regulatory requirements. While the response references "the EU AI Act" generically and acknowledges it as an official regulation, no specific articles, sections, official documents, or European Commission guidance is cited to support any individual claim.

### Common Pattern: Citation Missing Across All Claims
All 18 claims share the same fundamental problem:
- They make specific assertions about EU AI Act requirements
- They do not cite the specific article, section, or official source where these requirements appear
- The Act itself is a complex legislative text with multiple articles, annexes, and schedules
- Readers cannot verify which specific provisions are being referenced

### Why This is Problematic
The EU AI Act is a published, official EU regulation with:
- Specific articles (e.g., Article 6 on high-risk classification)
- Specific annexes listing high-risk categories
- Official European Commission guidance documents
- Specific implementation timelines

**Each claim should cite the specific article or annex.** For example:
- ✗ "High-risk AI systems include critical infrastructure" (no citation)
- ✓ "Article 6 and Annex I of the EU AI Act classify critical infrastructure as high-risk" (proper citation)

### Major Failure Mode
**Regulatory Claims Without Legal Citations:** The response provides what appears to be an authoritative summary of EU AI Act requirements but cites no official sources. This is particularly problematic for regulatory content because:
1. Regulations are official, citable documents
2. Specific requirements appear in specific articles
3. Misrepresenting regulatory requirements has legal consequences
4. Readers need to trace claims to official sources

### Strongest and Weakest Examples
- **Most problematic claim:** "High-risk AI systems require a conformity assessment and affixation of the CE mark" — This is a significant assertion about a new EU regulatory framework. CE marking for AI is a notable requirement that deserves specific Act reference.
- **Least problematic claim:** "The EU AI Act is an evolving regulation" — This is somewhat self-evident about legislation, though timeline claims still need sources.

### Accuracy Note
While this evaluation focuses on citations (not accuracy), it's worth noting that many of the claims appear consistent with the general content of the EU AI Act. However, **plausibility and accuracy are separate from citation quality.** A claim can be accurate and still lack proper citations.
